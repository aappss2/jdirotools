Reverse Mortgage: What it is and how to estimate your available equity

Reverse mortgages let qualifying homeowners convert part of their home equity into cash without selling their home or making monthly mortgage payments. Typically aimed at seniors, a reverse mortgage can provide a lump sum, steady monthly payments (tenure), or a line of credit that you can draw from as needed. The loan balance grows over time as interest and fees accrue, and repayment usually occurs when the borrower moves out permanently or the home is sold. This page gives a practical, easy-to-use estimate you can refine with a lender. The estimate below is educational and not a guarantee of financing.

Who qualifies?

Qualification rules vary by country, but most programs require the homeowner to be at least 62 years old (in the U.S. for HECM) and own their home. Lenders check the remaining mortgage balance, home value, and whether property taxes and insurance are current. Many programs require counseling to ensure borrowers understand costs and alternatives.

Main payout types

Lump sum: Receive a large one-time payment. Good for paying off debts, medical bills, or large expenses. Often results in higher interest accrual because the entire available amount begins accruing interest immediately.

Tenure: Receive fixed monthly payments for life (or a fixed number of years depending on program). Provides predictable income but may reduce available credit growth.

Line of credit: Access funds flexibly. Often the most powerful option because unused credit can grow over time and be available later when needed.

How lenders estimate available funds

Lenders use a Principal Limit Factor (PLF) tied to the borrower's age and current interest rates. Older borrowers generally qualify for a higher PLF because the expected loan period is shorter. Interest rates also affect the PLF: higher rates generally reduce the upfront amount. Our calculator uses a simplified PLF approximation to create a quick, transparent estimate you can use for planning.

Things to remember

  • Reverse mortgage interest compounds — loan balance increases over time.
  • Borrowers remain responsible for property taxes, insurance, and home maintenance.
  • When the loan becomes due, heirs can choose to repay the loan and keep the home or sell the property to repay the loan.

When a reverse mortgage makes sense

It can be a solid option when a homeowner needs to cover retirement expenses, medical costs, or wishes to delay taking other assets. It is less attractive if you want to leave the home as a large inheritance without mortgage obligations, or if you plan to move soon — fees and closing costs may offset short-term advantages.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q: Do I have to repay a reverse mortgage while I live in the home?
A: No — most plans require no monthly principal and interest payments. However, you must keep up with property taxes, home insurance and maintenance. The loan comes due if you permanently move out, sell the house, or fail to meet loan obligations.
Q: How is the available amount determined?
A: Lenders use your home's value, your age, current interest rates and existing mortgage balance. We show a simplified calculation here; exact offers require a lender evaluation.
Q: Can my heirs keep the home?
A: Yes — heirs may repay the loan balance (or refinance) to keep the home. Otherwise, the home can be sold to repay the loan and any remaining equity distributed to heirs.
Q: Are reverse mortgages safe?
A: They are regulated financial products in many countries and can be useful but complex. Counseling from a qualified advisor is usually mandatory for federally-insured programs.
