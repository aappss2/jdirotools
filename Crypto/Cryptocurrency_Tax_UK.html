How Cryptocurrency Is Taxed in the UK: A Complete HMRC Guide for 2025/26

Cryptocurrency taxation in the United Kingdom has become one of the most discussed financial topics in recent years. As more individuals invest in Bitcoin, Ethereum, and other digital assets, understanding your tax obligations is absolutely critical. HM Revenue & Customs (HMRC) has established clear guidelines on how crypto assets are taxed, and failing to comply can result in penalties, interest charges, and even criminal investigation.

This comprehensive guide — developed alongside our free crypto tax calculator UK tool — covers everything you need to know about paying tax on cryptocurrency in the United Kingdom. Whether you're a casual investor, active trader, or someone earning crypto through staking and mining, this resource will help you navigate the rules confidently. HMRC Classification: Crypto as Property, Not Currency

One of the most fundamental aspects of UK crypto taxation is that HMRC does not classify cryptocurrency as money or currency. Instead, crypto assets are treated as property — similar to shares, real estate, or other investment assets. This classification has significant tax implications because it means that virtually every transaction involving cryptocurrency can trigger a taxable event.

Taxable events include selling crypto for pounds or any fiat currency, exchanging one cryptocurrency for another (for example, swapping Bitcoin for Ethereum), using crypto to purchase goods or services, and gifting crypto to someone other than your spouse or civil partner. Each of these events requires you to calculate whether a gain or loss has been realised and report it accordingly.

Capital Gains Tax Rules for Cryptocurrency

When you dispose of cryptocurrency at a profit, you are liable for Capital Gains Tax (CGT). The gain is calculated as the difference between your acquisition cost (what you paid for the crypto, including any fees) and the disposal proceeds (what you received when you sold or exchanged it). Trading fees and transaction costs can be deducted from your gains, which is why our bitcoin tax calculator UK includes a field for fees.

The CGT rate you pay depends on your overall income tax band. Basic rate taxpayers pay 10% on crypto gains, while higher rate and additional rate taxpayers pay 20%. It is important to note that even if you are normally a basic rate taxpayer, your crypto gains could push you into the higher rate band if they are substantial enough. In such cases, part of your gains may be taxed at 10% and the remainder at 20%.

Annual Tax-Free Allowance (CGT Allowance)

Every UK taxpayer has an annual Capital Gains Tax-free allowance, known as the Annual Exempt Amount. For the 2025/26 tax year, this allowance is £3,000. This means the first £3,000 of your total capital gains — from all sources, not just cryptocurrency — is tax-free.

It is worth noting that this allowance has been reduced significantly in recent years. In 2022/23, it was £12,300, and it was halved to £6,000 in 2023/24 before being cut again to £3,000 from 2024/25 onwards. This reduction means many more crypto investors now find themselves with a tax liability, making tools like our crypto tax estimator UK 2026 more important than ever. The allowance cannot be carried forward — if you do not use it in a tax year, it is lost.

Income Tax on Crypto: Trading, Mining, and Staking

Not all crypto income falls under Capital Gains Tax. HMRC considers certain crypto activities as generating income rather than capital gains. If you receive cryptocurrency as payment for services, through an employer as salary, from mining activities, from staking rewards, from airdrops that are provided in return for a service, or through decentralised finance (DeFi) lending interest, then the value of the crypto received at the point of receipt is treated as income and taxed at your marginal Income Tax rate.

For example, if you stake Ethereum and receive rewards worth £500, that £500 is added to your income for the tax year. When you later sell those staked tokens, any increase in value since you received them is subject to CGT. This creates a two-layer tax situation that many investors overlook. The acquisition cost for CGT purposes is the value at which you reported the Income Tax.

Record Keeping Requirements

HMRC requires you to keep detailed records of all your cryptocurrency transactions. This is arguably the most challenging aspect of crypto tax compliance, especially for active traders who may execute hundreds or thousands of trades per year. The records you must maintain include the type of crypto asset, the date of each transaction, whether the transaction was a buy, sell, exchange, or other disposal, the quantity of tokens involved, the value in pounds sterling at the time of the transaction, cumulative totals of tokens held, and any fees or costs associated with the transaction.

You are legally required to keep these records for at least twelve months after the Self Assessment filing deadline. In practice, it is wise to keep records for much longer — HMRC can open investigations going back up to twenty years in cases of suspected fraud. Most crypto exchanges allow you to download your transaction history in CSV format, which provides a useful starting point. However, if you have used multiple exchanges or moved tokens to private wallets, compiling a complete record can be complex.

Pooling Rules: The Section 104 Holding

HMRC uses a pooling method known as Section 104 to calculate the cost basis of your crypto holdings. Under this rule, all tokens of the same type are treated as part of a single pool. The average cost of the pool is recalculated every time you acquire additional tokens. When you dispose of tokens, the cost basis used for calculating your gain or loss is the pooled average cost at that point.

For example, if you buy 1 Bitcoin at £20,000 and later buy another 0.5 Bitcoin at £30,000, your pool contains 1.5 Bitcoin with a total cost of £35,000. The average cost per Bitcoin is therefore £23,333.33. If you then sell 0.5 Bitcoin, your cost basis for that sale would be 0.5 multiplied by £23,333.33, equalling £11,666.67. However, before applying the Section 104 pool, HMRC requires you to first check whether the same-day rule or the bed and breakfast rule applies.

The Bed and Breakfast Rule (30-Day Rule)

The bed and breakfast rule — also known as the 30-day rule — is designed to prevent investors from selling and immediately rebuying assets solely to crystallise a tax loss while maintaining essentially the same investment position. Under this rule, if you sell cryptocurrency and repurchase the same type of token within 30 days, the acquisition cost used for calculating your gain or loss is the cost of the repurchased tokens, not the original pooled cost.

The matching rules are applied in a specific order of priority. First, any tokens acquired on the same day as the disposal are matched. Second, any tokens acquired within 30 days after the disposal are matched (the bed and breakfast rule). Third, tokens from the Section 104 pool are used. This ordering is important and can significantly affect your calculated gains. Understanding these rules is essential for anyone using a capital gains tax crypto UK strategy to minimise their tax bill legally.

Reporting on Your Self Assessment Tax Return

If you have capital gains from crypto that exceed the annual allowance, or if your total disposal proceeds exceed four times the annual allowance (£12,000 for 2025/26), you must report them on a Self Assessment tax return. You will need to complete the Capital Gains Tax summary pages (SA108) of the SA100 tax return. Even if your gains fall within the annual allowance, you are still required to report if your total proceeds exceed the four-times threshold.

The Self Assessment deadline for the 2025/26 tax year (which runs from 6 April 2025 to 5 April 2026) is 31 January 2027 for online returns. Paper returns have an earlier deadline of 31 October 2026. Late filing can result in automatic penalties starting at £100, with additional daily penalties for prolonged non-compliance. Interest is also charged on any tax paid late.

Loss Reporting and Carry Forward

If you dispose of cryptocurrency at a loss, that loss can be used to offset capital gains in the same tax year, reducing your overall CGT bill. If your losses exceed your gains, the unused losses can be carried forward indefinitely and set against gains in future tax years. However, to carry forward losses, you must report them to HMRC within four years of the end of the tax year in which the loss was made.

This is a commonly missed planning opportunity. Many investors who made losses during crypto market downturns in 2022 and 2023 can still report those losses and use them to reduce tax bills on future gains. It is also worth noting that losses on crypto investments that have become worthless (so-called negligible value claims) can sometimes be reported without an actual disposal if you can demonstrate the asset has become essentially valueless.

Compliance Tips for UK Crypto Investors

Staying compliant with HMRC crypto tax rules does not have to be overwhelming. Here are some practical tips to help you manage your obligations effectively:

  • Track everything from day one. Start recording your transactions as soon as you begin trading. Retrospective record-keeping is far more difficult and error-prone.
  • Use crypto tax software. Tools like Koinly, CryptoTaxCalculator, or TokenTax can connect to your exchange accounts and wallets to automatically compile transaction records and calculate gains using HMRC rules.
  • Do not forget crypto-to-crypto swaps. Trading one cryptocurrency for another is a taxable event. Many investors mistakenly believe that tax is only due when converting back to pounds.
  • Understand the 30-day rule. Be careful about wash sales — selling and rebuying within 30 days will not allow you to crystallise a loss effectively.
  • Report proactively. HMRC is increasingly sophisticated in tracking crypto. They have sent thousands of nudge letters and have data-sharing agreements with major exchanges. Voluntary disclosure is always preferable to being investigated.
  • Consider professional advice. If your crypto portfolio is substantial or your trading activity is complex, investing in professional tax advice from a crypto-specialist accountant can save significant money and stress.
