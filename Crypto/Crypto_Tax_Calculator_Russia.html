Russia Crypto Tax Rules 2026: Federal Tax Service Guidance

The Russian cryptocurrency market continues to evolve in 2026. Investors, traders, and miners are increasingly required to align their reporting and documentation with the expectations of the Russian Federal Tax Service (FNS). This guide explains the legal position of digital assets, how income is taxed, and what Russian residents and non-residents should consider when preparing declarations. It is written in clear language but reflects the professional level of detail needed for compliance.

Legal status of cryptocurrency in Russia

Cryptocurrency is recognized in Russia as a form of property rather than legal tender. That means you cannot use Bitcoin or other digital assets as official payment for goods and services in the domestic economy, but you can legally own, trade, and transfer them. The law on digital financial assets frames crypto as an asset class subject to taxation when gains are realized. As of 2026, Russian regulators have increased their focus on transparency, especially for large transactions and cross-border flows. This classification as property brings crypto income under general tax rules for individuals.

Federal Tax Service (FNS) overview

The Federal Tax Service is the central authority responsible for tax administration in Russia. The FNS uses bank data, foreign account reporting, and analytics to identify taxable income. For crypto, the FNS expects taxpayers to declare profits, show transaction history, and explain how amounts were calculated. While there is still a developing regulatory environment, the general principle is consistent: if you receive income in the form of cryptocurrency and convert it or dispose of it at a profit, the income is taxable under personal income tax rules.

Income tax rules for crypto gains

The tax base is generally the difference between the sale value and the acquisition cost, plus allowable expenses such as trading fees. The calculator above uses a simplified approach that mirrors common practices: total sale amount minus total purchase amount and fees. In practice, you should preserve exchange statements, wallet history, invoices, and other supporting evidence that demonstrate your cost basis. This is important because FNS may request evidence for how you computed your taxable income in the 3-NDFL declaration.

Resident vs non-resident taxation

Russia applies different rates based on tax residency. A resident is generally an individual who spends at least 183 days in Russia within a 12-month period. Residents usually pay 13% on annual income up to a specified threshold and 15% on income above that threshold. For the purposes of this estimator, a simplified rate applies: 13% on income up to the threshold and 15% above. Non-residents are subject to a flat 30% tax on many forms of Russian-sourced income. When evaluating crypto profits, it is vital to confirm your residency status because it directly affects your effective tax rate and your filing obligations.

Reporting requirements and declaration obligations

Cryptocurrency income is typically reported in the annual 3-NDFL declaration. If you dispose of crypto at a profit, you should include the income in the relevant tax period. The declaration usually must be submitted in the year following the income event, with taxes due by the statutory deadline. It is also common for the FNS to require evidence of the transactions. You can use exchange receipts, blockchain explorers, and bank statements to tie your sales proceeds to actual digital asset disposals. The more complete your records, the easier it is to support your declaration if questions arise.

Foreign asset reporting considerations

If you hold crypto on foreign exchanges or have wallets connected to international platforms, additional reporting may be relevant. Russia has rules for foreign account and foreign asset disclosures. While the rules do not always mention crypto explicitly, the FNS expects taxpayers to declare foreign holdings when required. This is especially important for high-value accounts, because failure to report can carry penalties. In 2026, cross-border compliance remains a central theme, and taxpayers should review whether their crypto platforms qualify as foreign financial assets.

Mining and staking taxation

Mining and staking can create taxable income at the time you receive the digital assets. For miners, the fair market value of coins when they are generated can be treated as income. Staking rewards may be treated similarly. Later, when you dispose of the assets, any additional gain or loss may be taxable based on the difference between the disposal value and the previously recorded income value. This layered approach means record keeping is essential. If you are operating mining equipment as a business or engaged in consistent staking activities, professional advice can help determine whether additional business tax obligations apply.

Record keeping requirements

The most important compliance practice is documentation. Maintain a ledger of dates, amounts, wallet addresses, and exchange rates at the time of each transaction. Keep screenshots or export files from exchanges showing the trade details. Store your conversion rates for crypto to RUB on the transaction date. The FNS values traceability, so clear documentation reduces the risk of disputes. By building a solid audit trail you can demonstrate how you calculated your taxable income and deductions.

Compliance tips for 2026

First, confirm your tax residency status well before the filing season. Second, organize your transaction records so you can calculate gains and losses accurately. Third, assess whether any of your crypto platforms are foreign exchanges that require additional reporting. Fourth, reconcile your bank inflows and outflows with exchange activity to avoid unexplained gaps. Finally, plan for cash flow to ensure taxes can be paid on time. Frequently Asked Questions

Is cryptocurrency legal in Russia? ⌄

Yes. Crypto is treated as property and can be owned and traded, but it is not legal tender for domestic payments.

How much tax do I pay on Bitcoin in Russia? ⌄

Residents generally pay 13% up to the threshold and 15% above it, while non-residents typically pay 30% on taxable gains.

Do residents and non-residents pay different tax? ⌄

Yes. Tax residency determines whether you apply the resident progressive rate or the non-resident flat rate.

Do I need to declare crypto income? ⌄

Yes, profits from crypto disposals are generally reported in the 3-NDFL declaration along with supporting documentation.

Is mining taxable? ⌄

Mining and staking rewards can be taxable as income when received, with further gains or losses on disposal.

Can crypto losses reduce taxes? ⌄

Losses may offset taxable income depending on how they are documented and classified. Keep detailed records.
