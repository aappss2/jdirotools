Complete Guide to Cryptocurrency Taxation in Israel (2026)

Legal Status of Cryptocurrency in Israel

Cryptocurrency is legal in Israel, though it is not classified as legal tender. The Israeli government and its regulatory bodies have taken a pragmatic approach to digital assets, recognizing their growing importance in the global financial landscape. As of 2026, Israeli residents are free to buy, sell, trade, and hold cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin, Ethereum, and other digital tokens. However, this freedom comes with clear tax obligations that every crypto holder must understand.

Israel's legal framework for crypto has evolved significantly since the early days of Bitcoin. The Bank of Israel does not recognize cryptocurrency as currency, and the Israel Securities Authority (ISA) has provided guidance indicating that certain tokens may be classified as securities depending on their characteristics. For the vast majority of retail investors, however, cryptocurrencies are treated as taxable assets—similar to stocks, bonds, or real estate.

The country has also implemented anti-money laundering (AML) regulations that apply to cryptocurrency exchanges and service providers operating within its borders. Israeli crypto exchanges must register with relevant authorities and comply with Know Your Customer (KYC) requirements, ensuring that the ecosystem remains transparent and accountable.

Role of the Israel Tax Authority (ITA)

The Israel Tax Authority (רשות המסים בישראל) is the government body responsible for tax collection and enforcement. In a landmark circular issued in 2018, the ITA officially clarified its position on cryptocurrency taxation, establishing that digital currencies are considered "assets" under the Israeli Income Tax Ordinance. This classification has remained the foundation of crypto tax policy through 2026.

The ITA's classification means that any profit derived from selling, exchanging, or otherwise disposing of cryptocurrency triggers a taxable event. The authority has also made it clear that crypto-to-crypto trades are taxable—meaning that swapping Bitcoin for Ethereum, for example, is not a tax-free transaction. Each disposal must be reported, and gains or losses calculated accordingly.

In recent years, the ITA has increased its enforcement capabilities, partnering with blockchain analytics firms to identify unreported crypto transactions. Israeli banks are also required to report suspicious activities related to cryptocurrency deposits, making it increasingly difficult for taxpayers to avoid their obligations. The message is clear: compliance is not optional.

Understanding the 25% Capital Gains Tax Rate

For individual investors—those who buy and hold cryptocurrency as a personal investment rather than as part of a business—the primary tax rate is 25% on capital gains. This rate applies to the net profit from selling crypto assets, calculated as the difference between the sale price and the original purchase price (cost basis), minus allowable deductions such as trading fees.

This 25% rate is consistent with the capital gains tax applied to other financial assets in Israel, including stocks and bonds. It's important to note that this rate applies to Israeli tax residents on their worldwide crypto gains, regardless of where the exchange is located or where the transaction takes place. An Israeli citizen trading on Binance, Coinbase, or any international platform is still subject to this tax.

The calculation is straightforward: if you purchased Bitcoin for ₪50,000 and sold it for ₪80,000, your gross gain is ₪30,000. After subtracting any trading fees (say ₪500), your net taxable gain is ₪29,500. At 25%, your estimated tax liability would be ₪7,375. Your net after-tax profit would be ₪22,125.

Business Taxation Rules for Crypto Traders

When cryptocurrency trading is conducted as a business activity—characterized by high frequency of trades, significant volume, use of sophisticated tools, or being the taxpayer's primary source of income—the profits are classified as business income rather than capital gains. This distinction carries significant tax implications.

Business income is taxed at the individual's marginal income tax rate, which ranges from 10% to 50% in Israel's progressive tax system. For high-earning traders, this can mean paying nearly double the rate that a passive individual investor would pay. Additionally, business traders may be subject to National Insurance (Bituach Leumi) and Health Insurance contributions on their crypto profits.

The ITA evaluates several factors to determine whether a taxpayer's crypto activity constitutes a business: the frequency and volume of transactions, the holding period (short-term vs. long-term), whether the taxpayer has other sources of income, the level of expertise and organization involved, and whether the activity is conducted with the intent to generate ongoing profits. There is no single bright-line test, and each case is evaluated on its merits.

Mining and Staking Taxation

Cryptocurrency mining—the process of using computational power to validate blockchain transactions in exchange for rewards—is also taxable in Israel. Mined tokens are generally treated as income at the time they are received, valued at their fair market value on the date of receipt. For individual miners, this may be classified as either business income or other income, depending on the scale and nature of the mining operation.

Similarly, staking rewards—earned by locking up cryptocurrency to support proof-of-stake blockchain networks—are subject to taxation. The ITA views staking rewards as a form of income, analogous to interest or dividends. The tokens received must be reported and valued at the time of receipt. When staked tokens are subsequently sold, any additional gain or loss from the change in value between receipt and sale is treated as a separate capital gains event.

For both mining and staking, deductible expenses may include electricity costs, hardware depreciation, and other directly related expenses if the activity is classified as a business. Proper record-keeping is essential to substantiate these deductions in the event of an audit.

Reporting Crypto in Your Annual Tax Return

Israeli taxpayers are required to report all cryptocurrency transactions in their annual tax return (דוח שנתי). This includes not only sales that result in a gain but also transactions that result in a loss, as well as any income from mining, staking, airdrops, or other crypto-related activities. Failure to report can result in penalties, interest charges, and in serious cases, criminal prosecution.

The annual tax return requires detailed information for each transaction, including the date of purchase, date of sale, amounts in ILS, the exchange or platform used, and the calculated gain or loss. For taxpayers with a large number of transactions, it may be advisable to use crypto tax software to aggregate and organize this data before filing.

Tax returns in Israel are generally due by April 30 of the following year for salaried employees, and by May 31 for self-employed individuals (though extensions may be granted). Crypto gains should be reported on the appropriate schedules, and advance tax payments (מקדמות) may be required if gains are substantial.

VAT Considerations for Businesses

Value Added Tax (VAT) is an important consideration for businesses engaged in cryptocurrency activities. In Israel, the standard VAT rate is 18% (as of 2026). The application of VAT to cryptocurrency transactions has been a subject of ongoing discussion.

Generally, the sale of cryptocurrency itself—when treated as a financial asset—is exempt from VAT, similar to the treatment of other financial instruments. However, services related to cryptocurrency, such as exchange services, consulting, or mining-as-a-service, may be subject to VAT. Businesses providing crypto-related services should carefully evaluate their VAT obligations and consult with a tax professional.

For crypto businesses registered as Licensed Dealers (Osek Mursheh) or Exempt Dealers (Osek Patur), understanding the interplay between income tax and VAT is critical to ensuring full compliance and avoiding unexpected liabilities.

Loss Carry-Forward Rules

One of the more favorable aspects of Israeli tax law for crypto investors is the ability to carry forward capital losses. If you sell cryptocurrency at a loss, that loss can be used to offset capital gains from other sources in the same tax year. This includes gains from stocks, bonds, real estate, and other capital assets—not just cryptocurrency.

If your total capital losses exceed your total capital gains in a given year, the excess loss can be carried forward to future tax years to offset future capital gains. This carry-forward provision is particularly valuable during bear markets when many investors realize significant losses. However, capital losses cannot be used to offset ordinary income (such as salary)—they can only offset capital gains.

Proper documentation of losses is essential. Taxpayers must maintain records of the original purchase price, sale price, dates, and any associated fees for each losing transaction. Without adequate documentation, the ITA may disallow the loss deduction.

Compliance Risks and Enforcement

The ITA has significantly ramped up its enforcement of cryptocurrency tax compliance in recent years. Using advanced blockchain analytics, data-sharing agreements with international tax authorities, and mandatory reporting from Israeli financial institutions, the authority is increasingly capable of identifying taxpayers who fail to report their crypto activities.

Penalties for non-compliance can be severe. Underreporting income may result in a penalty of up to 30% of the underpaid tax, plus interest. In cases of intentional evasion, criminal penalties including fines and imprisonment are possible. The ITA has also conducted voluntary disclosure programs in the past, allowing taxpayers to come forward and regularize their crypto tax obligations with reduced penalties.

Given these risks, proactive compliance is strongly recommended. Taxpayers with unreported crypto transactions should consider consulting a tax attorney or accountant to explore their options for voluntary disclosure before the ITA discovers the discrepancy.

Practical Example Calculation

Let's walk through a detailed example. Suppose Sarah, an Israeli individual investor, purchased 0.5 Bitcoin on January 15, 2025, for ₪75,000. She paid ₪375 in trading fees on the purchase. On March 1, 2026, she sold her 0.5 Bitcoin for ₪120,000, paying ₪600 in trading fees on the sale.

Her calculation would be as follows: The total cost basis is ₪75,000 (purchase) + ₪375 (buy fee) + ₪600 (sell fee) = ₪75,975. Her gross sale proceeds are ₪120,000. Her net capital gain is ₪120,000 − ₪75,975 = ₪44,025. At the 25% individual capital gains rate, her estimated tax is ₪11,006.25. Her net after-tax profit is ₪44,025 − ₪11,006.25 = ₪33,018.75. Her effective tax rate on the gross gain is approximately 25%.

If Sarah were classified as a business trader in the 47% tax bracket, her tax on the same transaction would be ₪44,025 × 47% = ₪20,691.75—nearly double the individual rate. This example illustrates why the individual vs. business classification is so consequential.
